# Warm-Up and Mobility

A warm-up prepares the body for what is about to happen, and mobility work maintains the range
of motion that lets movements happen well. They overlap in practice and are worth treating
together, because the modern version of a good warm-up is largely mobility work performed with
intent. Neither needs to be long or complicated: five to ten minutes of appropriate movement
covers most sessions. This page describes general practice for healthy adults and is not a
programme for restoring range of motion lost to an injury, which needs individual assessment.

## What a warm-up is for

A warm-up raises muscle temperature, which increases the speed of contraction and makes tissue
more compliant; it increases blood flow to the muscles about to work; and it wakes up the
coordination between nervous system and muscle, which is why the third set of an exercise
usually feels better than the first. It also serves a psychological function that is easy to
dismiss and genuinely useful, moving attention from whatever preceded the session to the
session itself.

Whether warming up prevents injury is less settled than the confident claims suggest. The
evidence is strongest for structured programmes in sport — the multi-part routines used in
football and similar contexts show reasonably consistent reductions in lower-limb injuries —
and much weaker for the general idea that a few minutes of movement before a gym session
protects you. Performance improvement, on the other hand, is straightforward: warm muscle
produces more force. That alone justifies the five minutes.

## A general warm-up

Five to ten minutes is enough for most sessions, and the structure is simple. Begin with a few
minutes of easy whole-body activity to raise temperature and heart rate — walking briskly,
cycling gently, rowing easily, marching on the spot. The marker is light breathlessness and a
sense of warmth, not fatigue. Cold weather and early mornings warrant a little longer, since
you are starting from further away.

Follow that with movements resembling what the session contains, at low intensity, through a
full range of motion. Before a lower-body session that means body-weight squats, hip hinges,
lunges, and leg swings; before upper-body work, arm circles, band pull-aparts, and push-ups
against a wall or a bench. Before lifting, the most efficient warm-up is the lift itself:
progressively heavier sets of the movement you are about to do, starting light. Before running,
a walk building to an easy jog with a few gentle strides does the job.

## Dynamic and static stretching

Dynamic stretching means moving through a range of motion under control — leg swings, arm
circles, walking lunges, hip circles, torso rotations — and it is the appropriate choice before
activity, because it warms tissue while rehearsing the range you are about to use. Static
stretching means holding a lengthened position still, and it is better suited to after a
session or a separate slot in the day, when improving flexibility is the goal in its own right.

The reason for that ordering is a real and frequently overstated finding. Long static holds
immediately before heavy or explosive work — holds beyond about sixty seconds are where the
effect becomes reasonably consistent — can transiently reduce force and power output. The effect
is modest, short-lived, and mostly irrelevant to someone stretching briefly before a walk, so
the sensible reading is not that static stretching is harmful but that it is the wrong tool for
the minutes before a heavy lift or a sprint.

## Mobility and range of motion

Mobility is usable range of motion: the range you can move through actively, under your own
control, rather than the range a limb can be pushed into by someone else. That distinction is
why strength at the end of a range matters as much as length, and why mobility work usually
looks like controlled movement rather than passive holding. The areas that tend to lose range
in people who sit a lot are hips, ankles, thoracic spine, and shoulders.

Consistency beats duration by a wide margin. Five to ten minutes most days does more than a
long weekly session, and the work does not need a dedicated slot — it fits into a warm-up, into
breaks in the working day, or into the evening while doing something else. Full-range strength
training is itself excellent mobility work, which is part of why deep squats, overhead pressing,
and Romanian deadlifts feature in so many programmes. Pain, as opposed to the ordinary stretch
sensation, means stop and, if it persists, get it assessed.

## Cooling down

The cool-down has a smaller evidence base than its place in tradition implies. Gradually
reducing intensity at the end of hard cardiovascular work is sensible, mainly because stopping
abruptly after sustained hard effort can leave people light-headed as blood pools in the legs;
a few minutes of easy movement avoids that. The stronger claims — that cooling down prevents
soreness or speeds recovery — are not well supported, and stretching afterwards does not
reliably reduce next-day soreness either.

That is not an argument against doing it. A few minutes of easy walking and some gentle
stretching at the end of a session is pleasant, it is a natural slot for the flexibility work
most people otherwise skip, and it marks the end of training in a way that helps the transition
back to the rest of the day. Treat it as a low-cost habit with modest, mostly comfort-related
benefits rather than an essential step whose omission undoes the session.
