# Sleep Hygiene

Sleep hygiene is the set of everyday habits and environmental conditions that make good sleep
more likely. None of it is a treatment, and none of it replaces a conversation with a
clinician about persistent sleep problems. What it offers is a short list of levers most
people can adjust at little cost: when they get up, how much light they see and when, what
the bedroom is like, and what the last hour before bed contains. The effects are cumulative
rather than dramatic, so the useful question is not whether one change fixes a single bad
night, but whether a week of consistent conditions produces better nights on average.

## How much sleep adults need

Most adults need seven to nine hours of sleep a night, and that is where general guidance has
settled for adults between roughly eighteen and sixty-four. Individual need varies inside the
range: some people are reliably fine at seven hours, others are not themselves below eight and
a half. Need is also not the same as opportunity. Time in bed includes the minutes spent
falling asleep and any time awake during the night, so eight hours in bed is usually somewhat
less than eight hours asleep, and shortening time in bed to exactly the target tends to
backfire.

The practical test is how you feel and function during the day rather than what a number says.
Waking near the same time without an alarm, feeling alert through the morning, and not needing
to catch up heavily at the weekend all suggest the amount is about right. Needing several
extra hours on free days is a common sign of sleep accumulated short during the week.
Regularly sleeping far more than nine hours, or feeling unrefreshed no matter how long the
night, is worth raising with a clinician rather than solving with schedule adjustments.

## Keeping a consistent schedule

A fixed wake time is the single most useful habit in this list. The body clock is set mainly
by when light arrives and when you get up, so holding the wake time steady stabilises
everything downstream, including when you begin to feel sleepy in the evening. Bedtime is
better treated as a consequence than a target: if you rise at the same hour each day and get
light soon after, the sleepy signal tends to arrive at a workable time on its own, which is
far easier than trying to fall asleep on command at a chosen hour.

Weekend drift is the usual complication. Sleeping in by three hours on a Saturday shifts the
clock much the way a flight west would, which is why Sunday night often feels like insomnia
and Monday morning feels like jet lag. Keeping weekend wake times within about an hour of
weekday ones avoids most of that. If you are short on sleep, a nap earlier in the day costs
less than a long lie-in, because a nap does not move the clock the way a late morning does.

## Light, temperature, and a dark bedroom

Bright light in the morning and dim light in the evening are two halves of the same lever.
Getting outside for a short while after waking is usually more effective than any indoor
lighting, since daylight is far brighter than a lit room even under cloud. In the evening the
goal is the opposite: lower, warmer light and less of it, particularly in the hour or two
before bed. Screens matter mostly through their brightness and through what they keep you
doing, rather than through some unique property of the light they emit.

A bedroom that is cool, dark, and quiet is the standard recommendation, with the cool part
often put at around eighteen to nineteen degrees Celsius. Treat that as a starting point
rather than a rule, because bedding and personal preference shift it substantially. Darkness
is worth some effort: blackout curtains or an eye mask, and charging devices outside the room
so that nothing glows or buzzes. Where noise cannot be removed, steady background sound masks
intermittent noise better than silence does, because it is the sudden changes that wake people.

## Winding down before bed

A wind-down is a buffer, not a ritual with a correct form. Twenty to thirty minutes of
something undemanding — reading, tidying, a shower, gentle stretching, music — separates the
day from the attempt to sleep, and that transition is the part that tends to go wrong when
people move straight from work or a phone to lights out. Doing roughly the same things in
roughly the same order helps, because the sequence itself becomes a cue that sleep is next.

Lying in bed awake and frustrated is worth avoiding, since it teaches exactly the association
you do not want. Common advice is to get up after fifteen or twenty minutes of that, do
something quiet in dim light elsewhere, and return when sleepy. Keeping a notepad nearby helps
when the problem is a mind that starts planning, because writing the thought down is often
enough to stop rehearsing it. If sleeplessness is a pattern across weeks rather than an
occasional night, it is a clinician's question rather than a scheduling one.

## Caffeine, alcohol, and evening meals

Caffeine lingers longer than most people expect. Its half-life in adults is commonly put at
around five hours, which means a mid-afternoon coffee can still have a meaningful amount
circulating at bedtime, and sensitivity varies widely from person to person. Shifting caffeine
earlier in the day is a cheap experiment with a clear result, and someone who sleeps badly and
drinks coffee late in the afternoon is usually better off testing that before changing
anything else about their evenings.

Alcohol is the mirror image. It tends to shorten the time taken to fall asleep while making
the second half of the night lighter and more broken, so a nightcap often buys an easy start
at the cost of a worse night overall. Large meals close to bedtime can be uncomfortable enough
to interfere, though a light snack is not a problem for most people, and going to bed
genuinely hungry is not helpful either. These are general tendencies rather than rules that
hold for everyone, and they are worth testing individually.
