# Strength Training Fundamentals

Strength training means asking muscles to work against a resistance that is meaningful to
them, whether that resistance comes from dumbbells, a barbell, machines, bands, or body
weight. The fundamentals are the same in every case, and they are simpler than the volume of
available advice suggests: train each major muscle group a couple of times a week, cover the
main movement patterns, make the work gradually harder over months, and leave enough recovery
between sessions. Everything else is refinement. This page describes general training for
healthy adults, not a programme for any individual.

## Why strength training is worth the time

Adults lose muscle mass and strength gradually from around the fourth decade of life if
nothing pushes back, and resistance training is the most direct thing that pushes back. The
returns show up in ordinary function rather than only in appearance: carrying shopping,
climbing stairs, getting off the floor, keeping balance. Strength work also loads bone, which
is part of why it features in general guidance on maintaining bone density, and it tends to
improve how people feel about their bodies independently of what changes.

This is why major physical activity guidelines pair aerobic recommendations with muscle
strengthening on at least two days a week, treating the two as complementary rather than
alternatives. The threshold for benefit is low, and the first months of training in an
untrained person produce the steepest gains, most of them from the nervous system learning the
movements rather than from new muscle tissue. That is worth knowing, because it means early
progress is fast, and the slower phase afterwards is normal rather than a sign of failure.

## How often to train

Training each major muscle group at least twice a week is the general recommendation, and it
holds up well against the alternative of hitting everything once. Muscle responds to a
stimulus for something like a day or two afterwards, so two moderate sessions spread across
the week produce more than one long session that leaves you sore for four days. Two or three
full-body sessions a week suits most people who are not training for a sport, and it fits into
a schedule without much negotiation.

Leaving roughly forty-eight hours before training the same muscle group hard again is the usual
guidance, which is why full-body sessions are typically spaced rather than stacked on
consecutive days. Training a different area on the intervening days is fine, as is walking or
easy cardio. Sessions do not need to be long: forty-five minutes of focused work covering a
few compound movements does more than ninety minutes of drifting between machines, and short
sessions are far easier to keep doing.

## Choosing exercises

A small number of compound movements — ones that involve several joints and a lot of muscle at
once — covers almost everything. A useful way to organise them is by pattern: a squat, a hinge
such as a deadlift or hip thrust, a push such as a press or push-up, a pull such as a row or
assisted pull-up, and something for carrying or bracing, such as a loaded walk or a plank.
Covering those patterns twice a week is a complete programme for general strength.

Isolation exercises for a single muscle are not wrong, they are just lower priority: they add
detail once the compound work is in place. Equipment matters less than people expect. Machines
are easier to learn and useful when training alone, free weights demand more coordination and
transfer more directly to awkward real-world loads, and body-weight work needs nothing at all
and can be progressed by changing leverage. The best choice is the one you will actually use
twice a week.

## Sets, repetitions, and progressive overload

For general strength, two to four sets of each exercise is a sensible range, and a repetition
range of roughly eight to twelve is the standard suggestion for beginners because it balances
learning the movement against making the muscle work hard. Strength develops across a wide
range of repetitions, so precision is unnecessary; what matters is that the last few
repetitions of a set feel genuinely difficult while your form still holds. A set finished with
plenty in reserve provides little stimulus.

Progressive overload is the mechanism behind all of it: over weeks and months, the work has to
get harder. Add a small amount of weight, or another repetition, or another set, or slow the
lifting phase down, whenever the current version starts to feel manageable. Progress in
untrained people can be weekly at first and then becomes much slower, which is normal.
Recording what you lifted is the single most useful habit here, because memory reliably
overestimates last week and progression then stalls unnoticed.

## Form, starting weight, and pain

Start lighter than feels necessary. The first few sessions with any movement are for learning
the pattern, and a weight light enough to control teaches it faster than one that forces
compensation. Sound general cues are to keep the spine in a neutral position rather than
rounding under load, to move through a full range of motion you can control, to breathe rather
than hold your breath through heavy effort, and to stop a set when technique degrades instead
of grinding out one more repetition.

A few sessions with a qualified coach is the highest-value spend available to a new lifter,
particularly for loaded hinge movements. Muscle soreness in the day or two after a session is
ordinary, especially early on or after new exercises. Sharp pain during a movement, pain in a
joint rather than a muscle, and pain that persists or worsens are different, and the right
response is to stop that movement and have it assessed by a clinician or physiotherapist
rather than working through it or self-treating.
