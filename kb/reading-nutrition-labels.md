# Reading Nutrition Labels

A nutrition label is one of the few places in the food environment where the information is
standardised and reasonably honest, and reading one takes about fifteen seconds once you know
where to look. The skill is mostly knowing which parts are informative and which are
marketing. This page explains how the standard elements work in general terms. Exact formats
and thresholds differ between countries, and anyone told to monitor a specific nutrient for
health reasons should follow the guidance they were given.

## Start with the serving size

Every number in the nutrition panel refers to the stated serving size, which makes it the first
thing to read and the most frequently skipped. Serving sizes are set by convention or
regulation rather than by how people actually eat, so they are often smaller than a realistic
portion: a small bag of crisps may be listed as two servings, a bottle of drink as two and a
half, a tub of ice cream as four. Every figure on the panel then has to be multiplied to mean
anything.

Comparing two products means comparing at the same basis. Labels that give a per-hundred-gram
or per-hundred-millilitre column make this easy, and where both a per-serving and a
per-hundred-gram column exist, the latter is the honest one for comparison, because two brands
can declare different serving sizes for the same food. Checking servings per container also
catches the common surprise of a single-serving-looking package that officially contains three.

## Percent Daily Value

Percent Daily Value tells you how much one serving contributes to a day's reference intake of a
nutrient, and it exists to save you arithmetic. The general rule of thumb worth memorising is
that five percent Daily Value or less is low and twenty percent or more is high. That applies in
both directions: high is what you want for fibre, vitamins, and minerals, and low is what you
usually want for saturated fat, sodium, and added sugars.

Two caveats keep it in perspective. The percentages are based on a reference daily intake for a
notional adult, so they are not personalised, and individual needs vary. And not every nutrient
carries a percentage — some are listed in grams only, either because no reference intake has
been established or because the format does not require it. Used as a quick scan for what a
food is high and low in, though, the column answers most questions faster than the gram figures
beside it.

## The ingredients list

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight, which makes the first three or four the
most informative words on the package. When sugar, a syrup, or a refined oil leads the list, the
product is largely that thing regardless of what the front of the pack emphasises. When a
wholegrain flour leads on a bread, that tells you more about it than a wholesome-looking label
does.

Two habits are worth having. First, look for the same substance under several names, since
sugars appear as syrup, molasses, honey, concentrated fruit juice, and any number of words
ending in -ose, and splitting one sweetener across three entries pushes each further down the
list. Second, treat a long list as a signal about how reformulated a product is rather than as
inherently bad — some long lists are vitamins and spices. Allergens are typically emphasised in
bold or listed separately, and that section is the authoritative one for anyone avoiding an
ingredient.

## Sugars, sodium, and fats

Total sugars include both sugars naturally present, as in the lactose in milk or the fructose in
fruit, and those added during manufacture, which is why a plain yoghurt and a sweetened one can
look closer than they are. Where a label separates added sugars, that line is the more useful
one; where it does not, the ingredients list is the way to tell. Salt and sodium are two ways of
expressing the same thing and labels differ in which they use, so a product listing sodium is
not automatically lower in salt than one listing salt.

For fats, the saturated fat line is generally the one guidance focuses on, and the total fat
figure tells you less on its own — a food can be relatively high in total fat and largely
unsaturated, as nuts, olive oil, and oily fish are. Fibre is the line most people underuse:
scanning for a high fibre percentage is a fast way to compare breads, cereals, and crackers,
and it correlates reasonably well with how minimally processed the grain component is.

## Claims on the front of the pack

Front-of-pack claims are marketing operating within rules, which makes them technically true and
frequently uninformative. Regulated phrases such as low fat, reduced sugar, or high in fibre
have defined thresholds, but a threshold is not a verdict on the food: reduced sugar means less
than the comparable standard product, which may still be a great deal, and low fat products are
sometimes reformulated with more sugar to compensate. The claim tells you about one nutrient,
not about the product.

Unregulated language deserves less attention still. Natural, wholesome, artisan, and similar
words carry no fixed definition, and images of fields and grains are decoration. Front-of-pack
summary schemes, such as colour-coded traffic-light labels or single-letter grades, are a
genuine shortcut for quick comparison, and the panel on the back is where to check anything
that matters. As a general rule, the least processed foods carry the fewest claims, because
plain vegetables have no packaging to print them on.
