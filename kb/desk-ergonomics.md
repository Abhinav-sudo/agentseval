# Desk Ergonomics

Ergonomics is the practice of fitting the workspace to the person instead of the other way
round. The goal is not a single correct posture held rigidly for eight hours — no posture
tolerates that — but a setup that keeps joints in comfortable neutral positions and makes
frequent movement easy. Most of the improvement available at a desk comes from a handful of
adjustments that cost nothing. This page describes general setup principles; persistent pain,
numbness, or tingling is a reason to see a clinician rather than to keep adjusting furniture.

## Chair height and seated position

Start with the chair, because everything else is positioned relative to your body once it is
supported. Set the height so your feet rest flat on the floor with thighs roughly parallel to
the ground and knees at approximately ninety degrees, with a small gap between the back of the
knee and the seat edge. If the desk forces the chair higher than that, a footrest restores the
position; if the chair cannot go low enough, that is the first thing worth replacing.

Sit back into the chair so the backrest supports you, with the lumbar support meeting the
inward curve of the lower back — a rolled towel or small cushion does the same job on a chair
without one. Shoulders should be relaxed rather than shrugged or pulled forward, and armrests
should meet your elbows without lifting them. Reclining slightly, at around one hundred to one
hundred and ten degrees, is often more comfortable than bolt upright, and varying the position
through the day matters more than any single one.

## Monitor position

Two guidelines cover most monitor problems. The top of the screen should sit at or just below
eye level, so your gaze travels slightly downward and your neck stays neutral rather than
tipping back or craning forward. The screen should be roughly an arm's length away, commonly
put at fifty to seventy centimetres, adjusted for text size and eyesight. A screen too low is
the most common fault and the most reliable cause of neck and upper-back complaints.

Books or a stand raise a monitor that will not go high enough. Position the screen directly in
front of you rather than off to one side, since a persistent twist is worse than a slightly
awkward distance. With two monitors, put the primary one straight ahead and the secondary
beside it, angled inward, rather than splitting the difference and facing the gap between them.
Place the screen perpendicular to windows where possible, because glare from behind or in front
causes squinting and leaning that no amount of chair adjustment fixes.

## Keyboard, mouse, and wrists

Keep the keyboard close enough that your upper arms hang near vertical and your elbows stay at
roughly ninety degrees, with forearms approximately parallel to the floor. Wrists should be
straight and neutral, not bent up, down, or sideways, and should float rather than press hard
into a rest — a wrist rest is for pauses between typing, not a pivot to lean on. Those little
legs that tilt the back of a keyboard up generally push the wrists into extension and are best
left folded.

The mouse belongs immediately beside the keyboard at the same height, so reaching for it does
not require extending the arm or lifting the shoulder. A mouse too far away or too high is a
frequent and easily missed source of shoulder discomfort. Keeping the mouse sensitivity high
enough that small movements suffice reduces the total work, and shortcuts reduce it further.
Alternating hands, or switching between a mouse and a trackpad, spreads the load. Numbness or
tingling in the hands is a signal to stop adjusting and consult a clinician.

## Laptops and improvised workspaces

A laptop cannot be ergonomic on its own, because the screen and keyboard are fixed together:
position the screen correctly and the keyboard is too high, position the keyboard correctly and
the screen is too low. For anything beyond short stints the answer is to separate them — raise
the laptop on a stand or a stack of books to bring the screen to eye level, and add an external
keyboard and mouse at the proper height. That combination is inexpensive and resolves most of
the problem.

For temporary setups, aim for the closest available approximation and shorten the sessions.
Working at a kitchen table with a cushion for lumbar support and the laptop raised beats a
sofa. Working on a sofa or in bed is fine occasionally and poor as a default, since neither
supports the position for long. When travelling, changing location and posture every half hour
compensates for a setup you cannot fix, which is a general principle: movement substitutes
reasonably well for equipment.

## Movement breaks and eye strain

No posture is good enough to hold for hours, and the most consistent finding in this area is
that varying position matters more than achieving the ideal one. A common recommendation is to
stand or move for a minute or two at least every thirty minutes, and to take a longer break
away from the desk every hour or two. Pairing breaks to existing events — a finished task, a
phone call, the kettle — works better than relying on a timer people learn to dismiss.

A sit-stand desk helps mainly by making variation easy, and standing all day is its own problem
rather than a solution, so alternating is the point. For eyes, the widely cited advice is the
twenty-twenty-twenty rule: every twenty minutes, look at something about twenty feet away for
twenty seconds, which relaxes the focusing muscles. Blinking deliberately helps, since blink
rate drops substantially while staring at a screen, and persistent eye strain or headaches are
worth raising with an optometrist or clinician.
