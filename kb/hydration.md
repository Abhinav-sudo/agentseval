# Hydration

Hydration gets more attention than it usually deserves and less precision than it could
easily have. For most healthy adults going about an ordinary day, thirst does the job well,
and the interesting questions are narrower: what to do in heat, what to do around exercise,
and how to tell whether the amount you drink is roughly right. This page covers those general
cases. It does not address fluid needs that a clinician manages, and anyone who has been given
specific instructions about how much to drink should follow those instead of anything here.

## How much fluid people need

There is no single correct amount, because need depends on body size, activity, air
temperature, humidity, and how much fluid comes from food. General guidance tends to describe
a range of roughly two to three litres of total water a day for adults, and the word total is
what most summaries drop: that figure includes water from food and from every drink, not the
volume of plain water you are supposed to pour. Reported intakes vary enormously between
people who are all perfectly well hydrated, which is a good reason to treat any single number
as a rough anchor.

Thirst is a more useful instrument than arithmetic for most people. It responds to the same
things fluid need responds to, and it does so continuously, whereas a daily target cannot know
that today was hot or that you spent the afternoon walking. The main situations where thirst
alone is a weaker guide are prolonged exercise in heat, illness involving fluid loss, and old
age, since the thirst signal tends to blunt somewhat over the years. In those cases drinking
to a routine rather than purely to thirst is reasonable.

## The eight-glasses rule

The advice to drink eight glasses of water a day is the most repeated hydration claim there
is, and it has no clear basis in evidence. It appears to be a garbled restatement of older
guidance that described total daily water intake, including the substantial share that arrives
in food, which was then repeated as though it meant eight glasses of plain water on top of
everything else. It survives because it is memorable and roughly harmless, not because it was
ever established.

Treating it as a target has two small costs. It makes people who drink less than eight glasses
believe they are dehydrated when nothing suggests they are, and it makes some people drink
well past thirst on the theory that more must be better. More is not better. Beyond the point
where you are drinking to comfortable satiety, extra fluid is simply extra trips to the
bathroom, and pushing very large volumes during endurance events without regard to thirst is a
genuine risk rather than a cautious choice.

## Practical signs of adequate hydration

Urine colour is the most useful everyday check. Pale straw or light yellow generally indicates
adequate hydration, while a consistently dark yellow suggests drinking more, and completely
colourless urine usually means you are drinking more than you need. The check is imperfect,
since some foods, and some medicines a clinician has prescribed, change the colour
independently of hydration, so read it as one signal rather than a measurement.

Frequency and volume tell you something similar: most people pass urine several times across a
waking day, and a long stretch with none while active in the heat is worth acting on. Thirst,
a dry mouth, headache, and flagging concentration in the afternoon are common early signs that
fluid is low, though all of them have other causes and none of them is diagnostic. The
sensible response to any of these is a glass of water and a look at the day rather than
alarm — and persistent thirst that drinking does not settle is a reason to see a clinician.

## Fluids around exercise and in the heat

For ordinary sessions of under an hour in comfortable conditions, drinking to thirst before,
during, and after is enough, and water is the appropriate drink. Starting a session already
thirsty is the common mistake, so a glass in the half hour beforehand is a reasonable habit.
Weighing yourself before and after long sessions is the most direct way to see how much you
actually lose, since sweat rates differ several-fold between people and the same person in
different weather.

Heat and long duration change the picture. Sweat carries salt as well as water, so sessions
lasting well beyond an hour, especially hot ones, are where drinks containing sodium and some
carbohydrate start to make sense over plain water. Practical heat measures matter at least as
much as what is in the bottle: going out earlier or later in the day, slowing the pace,
choosing shade, and being willing to cut a session short. Anyone who becomes confused,
stops sweating, or feels faint in the heat needs immediate medical attention, not a drink.

## What counts as fluid

Almost everything you drink counts, including tea and coffee. The idea that caffeinated drinks
dehydrate you does not hold at ordinary intakes: they act as mild diuretics, but the fluid
they contain more than covers the additional loss, so a cup of coffee is a net gain of water.
Milk, juice, and soft drinks all hydrate too, though the sugar in some of them is a reason to
prefer water most of the time, and alcohol is the genuine exception because it promotes fluid
loss.

Food supplies a meaningful share of daily water, commonly estimated at around a fifth to a
quarter of total intake, and far more than that for someone eating plenty of fruit, vegetables,
soup, and yoghurt. That share is why plain-water targets are misleading in both directions:
someone eating a salad, a bowl of soup, and an orange has already taken in a surprising volume
before drinking anything. Spreading intake across the day is more comfortable than large
volumes at once, but nothing depends on the schedule being precise.
