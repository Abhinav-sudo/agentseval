# Cardio Basics

Cardiovascular exercise is any sustained activity that raises your heart rate and breathing:
walking briskly, cycling, swimming, running, rowing, dancing, climbing hills. The health
benefits are among the best established in medicine, and most of them accrue at intensities
far below what people imagine when they picture exercise. This page covers general aerobic
activity for healthy adults — how much, how hard, and how to build it up — and it is not
advice for anyone managing a heart or lung condition, which is a matter for their clinician.

## How much activity is recommended

The World Health Organization recommends that adults do at least 150 to 300 minutes of
moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity
activity, or an equivalent combination of the two, and additionally that they do
muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week. Those figures are the reference point
most national guidelines follow. Roughly two minutes of moderate activity is treated as
equivalent to one minute of vigorous activity, which is where the two ranges come from.

Two details in that guidance are routinely lost. The first is that the benefit curve is
steepest at the bottom: the largest single improvement in risk comes from moving from almost
nothing to a modest amount, so someone doing sixty minutes a week is getting a substantial
share of the available benefit and should not be told they are failing a target. The second is
that the old requirement for activity to come in bouts of at least ten minutes was dropped —
activity of any duration counts towards the total, including the short walks that make up an
ordinary day.

## Judging intensity

The talk test is the most practical intensity gauge and needs no equipment. At moderate
intensity you are breathing harder than usual and could hold a conversation but not sing; at
vigorous intensity you can manage only a few words at a time before needing a breath. Brisk
walking, easy cycling, and doubles tennis are typical moderate activities. Running, swimming
lengths, cycling fast or uphill, and singles tennis are typically vigorous, though the
classification depends on the person as much as the activity.

Rating your own effort works nearly as well: on a scale of one to ten where one is sitting
still and ten is an all-out sprint, moderate activity sits around four to six and vigorous
activity around seven to eight. Heart-rate zones from a watch are a reasonable additional
signal, but the common maximum-heart-rate formulas have a wide error margin across
individuals, so a zone that contradicts your breathing and perceived effort is more likely
wrong than you are. Uphill sections, heat, and humidity all raise the effort of the same pace.

## Building up gradually

The most common way to derail a return to exercise is to start at the volume you managed at
your fittest. Muscles and the cardiovascular system adapt within weeks, while tendons, bone,
and connective tissue adapt considerably more slowly, and most overuse problems in new runners
come from that mismatch rather than from anything about their technique. Beginning at a volume
that feels almost too easy and holding it for a fortnight before adding anything is a slower
start and a faster year.

Increasing total weekly volume by roughly ten percent at a time is the familiar rule of thumb.
It is not a precise finding, but the principle behind it — change one variable modestly, then
let it settle — is sound. Alternating harder days with easy ones or rest, and treating any
week that feels unusually heavy as a signal to repeat rather than advance, keeps progress
steady. Walk-run intervals are an effective on-ramp to running and are not a lesser version of
it.

## Mixing steady and harder efforts

Most of a week's aerobic work is best done at a comfortable, conversational pace. It is
sustainable, recovers quickly, and drives much of the adaptation, which is why endurance
athletes spend the large majority of their training there rather than in the middle ground
where sessions feel hard enough to be tiring but are not hard enough to be a real stimulus.
Building a base of easy work also makes the harder sessions possible.

Adding one or two harder sessions a week, once a base is established, improves fitness further
and takes less time. Intervals — repeated efforts of anywhere from thirty seconds to five
minutes with easy recovery between them — are the usual format, and hills do the same job with
less structure. One or two such sessions a week is plenty for general fitness, since the
recovery cost is real. Variety across activities also spreads the mechanical load, which is
why cycling or swimming alongside running tends to keep people healthier than running alone.

## When to stop and seek help

Some sensations during exercise mean stop now rather than push through. Chest pain, pressure,
or tightness; pain spreading to the arm, neck, or jaw; sudden severe breathlessness out of
proportion to the effort; dizziness or fainting; an irregular or racing heartbeat that does not
settle when you slow down — all of these need urgent medical attention, and the correct
response is emergency care, not a rest and a rescheduled session.

Less dramatic signals are worth respecting too. Unusual fatigue that lasts for days, a resting
heart rate that has drifted upward, sleep that has worsened, and performance sliding while
effort rises usually mean the load is too high for current recovery, and the answer is easier
weeks rather than more discipline. Anyone who has been inactive for a long time, is living with
a chronic condition, or is unsure whether an increase in activity is safe for them should
discuss it with a clinician first.
