# General Nutrition Principles

Nutrition advice is unusually noisy, and most of the noise concerns details that matter far
less than a handful of basics. This page sticks to the basics: the overall shape of a meal,
the difference minimal processing makes, why fibre and plant variety keep appearing in every
set of guidelines, and how to make any of it survive a normal week. It gives no calorie or
macronutrient targets and describes no particular diet, because those are individual questions
and, where health conditions are involved, questions for a dietitian or clinician.

## The plate model

The most durable piece of general guidance is a picture rather than a number. Fill about half
the plate with vegetables and fruit, weighted towards vegetables and towards variety; about a
quarter with a protein food; and about a quarter with a starchy food, preferably a whole grain.
It survives scrutiny because it changes proportions rather than prescribing amounts, so it
scales to a large appetite and a small one, adapts to most cuisines, and can be followed
without weighing anything or reading a label.

The model is a rough guide to a typical main meal, not a rule for every plate. A bowl of pasta
with vegetables stirred through it, a stir-fry, a curry with dal and rice, and a sandwich with
salad can all satisfy roughly the same proportions without looking like the diagram. Judged
across a week rather than a meal, most people find the vegetable share is the one that falls
short, which makes it the highest-yield thing to change. Eating more vegetables usually
displaces something more refined without any need to forbid it.

## Whole and minimally processed foods

Processing is a spectrum rather than a category, and the useful distinction is how far a food
has travelled from its recognisable form. Milled, frozen, tinned, and dried foods are lightly
processed and generally excellent: frozen vegetables and tinned beans and fish are convenient,
cheap, and nutritionally close to fresh, and dismissing them as processed is a mistake that
makes eating well harder than it needs to be. Wholemeal flour, plain yoghurt, and rolled oats
sit comfortably in the same group.

At the far end sit products reformulated to be highly palatable, typically energy-dense and
high in refined starch, sugar, salt, and fat, and easy to eat quickly and in quantity. Diets
built largely on those products are consistently associated with worse health outcomes. The
practical implication is not a ban but a default: cook from recognisable ingredients most of
the time, keep the convenient minimally processed staples on hand so that cooking stays
realistic on a bad evening, and let the rest be occasional rather than structural.

## Fibre and plant variety

Fibre appears in every set of dietary guidelines because the evidence behind it is unusually
consistent, linking higher intakes with better digestive health and lower risk of several
chronic diseases. Most people in high-income countries fall short of the recommended amount.
The foods that supply it are unglamorous and cheap: beans, lentils and other pulses, whole
grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts, and seeds. Getting fibre from those foods rather than from an
added powder means also getting the other things that come with them.

Variety appears to matter alongside quantity, and the common informal target is thirty
different plant foods a week, counting vegetables, fruit, whole grains, pulses, nuts, seeds,
and herbs and spices. The number is a prompt rather than a finding, but it is a good prompt,
because it pushes towards rotating what you buy instead of eating the same three vegetables.
Increasing fibre gradually and drinking enough fluid alongside it is worth doing, since a
sudden large increase is uncomfortable for most people.

## Protein and meal composition

Protein is worth some attention for reasons that have nothing to do with the physique
marketing attached to it: it is the most satiating of the macronutrients, and it supports
maintaining muscle, which matters increasingly with age and alongside any strength training.
Most people eating a varied diet with enough total food meet their needs without calculation.
Spreading protein across meals rather than concentrating it in the evening is a reasonable
default, mainly because it makes the earlier meals more satisfying.

Sources can be animal or plant, and a mix works well. Beans, lentils, tofu, tempeh, nuts,
seeds, and whole grains cover plant sources; eggs, dairy, fish, poultry, and meat cover the
rest. Building each meal around a protein food, a plant food or two, and a whole-grain or
starchy component is a simple template that removes most decisions. Anyone with a health
condition affecting what they should eat, or following a restricted diet for any reason,
should work that through with a dietitian rather than a general guide.

## Consistency over perfection

The pattern across weeks decides outcomes, not the composition of any single meal, and diets
fail far more often through unsustainability than through the wrong choice of foods. That
argues for changes you can imagine keeping: one more vegetable at dinner, a better default
breakfast, cooking twice as much and eating the remainder tomorrow. Practical friction is
usually the real obstacle, so a stocked cupboard of tins, frozen vegetables, and grains does
more for how someone eats than any amount of resolve.

Restriction that requires constant vigilance tends to collapse and take the good habits with
it. Framing food in moral terms makes that worse, since guilt is a poor motivator and an
excellent trigger for giving up entirely. Eating out, celebrating, and enjoying something
purely because it is enjoyable are all part of a sustainable pattern. If eating feels
controlling, distressing, or compulsive, that is worth discussing with a clinician, and it is
not something a general guide can address.
